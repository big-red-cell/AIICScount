cr...@chromium.org： <p>Thanks for chiming in and explaining your reasoning.  I&#39;ll walk through an example to show why the behavior in Chrome is correct and why the behavior in Firefox is incorrect, and I would actually encourage you to file a report with Mozilla to ensure they update their implementation to match, because it sounds like they may be vulnerable to a URL spoof.</p> <p>First, suppose an attacker hosts their own content on a long origin that doesn&#39;t entirely fit within the address bar: <code>https://login.your-bank.com.evil.com</code>.  When the address bar is narrow, Chrome ensures that the <code>evil.com</code> part of the URL is visible, because the registrable domain gives the best indication of where the content is coming from.  (The attacker cannot choose <code>your-bank.com</code> as their registrable domain if it has already been registered by the real <code>your-bank.com</code>.  In contrast, the attacker can easily create a <code>login.your-bank.com</code> subdomain within <code>evil.com</code>.) If Chrome showed the prefix of the URL (<code>https://login.your-bank.com</code>) instead of the registrable domain, it could be used for an effective URL spoof of <code>your-bank.com</code>.  This part is clearly documented in the URL display guidelines I mentioned earlier.</p> <p>Now suppose that the attacker creates a blob URL on the same page and opens a new window to it, as in the attacks described here.  If Chrome showed <code>blob:https://login.your-bank.com</code>, it could again be used as an effective URL spoof of <code>your-bank.com</code>.  The more important part is to show the registrable domain (<code>evil.com</code>), because that is a better indicator of who is providing the content.  In this sense, Firefox&#39;s behavior is at risk of URL spoofs, because it is not showing the registrable domain.</p> <p>While Chrome could make an effort to show the <code>blob:</code> prefix along with the registrable domain (<code>evil.com</code>), that is not necessary from a security perspective, because blob and non-blob URLs have the same origin and capabilities as each other.  Importantly, anything that a <code>blob:https://login.your-bank.com.evil.com</code> page can do can also be done directly by a non-blob <code>https://login.your-bank.com.evil.com</code> page.  For example, your attack involving form inputs could be done <strong>without using a blob URL</strong>, such as:</p> <pre><code>// Open a window with a `https://login.your-bank.com.evil.com` URL. w = window.open(location.href); // Rewrite the page in the new window to have a form asking for email and password, under the same URL. w.document.write(html_with_form_inputs); </code></pre> <p>It is important to show <code>evil.com</code> in the address bar of the new window, whether the window was created using a blob URL or not.</p> <p>In the Google Sites example, I&#39;ve previously explained that it is important to show <code>googleusercontent.com</code>.  That domain name was chosen to show that it contains &quot;user content&quot; and not official Google content like <code>google.com</code>, so it is intentional to show that and not the prefix of the URL.</p> <p>For these reasons, closing this as Working as Intended is the correct outcome.  I&#39;ll give you a chance to reply before closing, but as noted, I would encourage you to file a security report with Mozilla instead.</p>
